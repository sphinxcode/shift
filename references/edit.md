# EDIT: rewrite for standing

Run `references/clear.md` first.

## The three tests, in order

Ask them in this order. Most bad sentences die at the first one.

### 1. Should this sentence exist?

Cut before you rewrite. A polished sentence carrying nothing costs the reader
attention and gives nothing back.

Delete on sight:

- Anything the reader already knows. "Press Enter to send." "Click the button
  below to continue."
- Anything the interface already says. A heading restated as the first line
  underneath it. A button labelled Continue with "Click Continue" beside it.
- Anything true of every product. "Your data is important to us."
- Padding after a complete thought. "The plan is 70% less. It'll be here when
  you want it." The second sentence is politeness with nothing inside it.

**No rewrite saves a sentence that should not be there.**

### 2. Where does it leave the reader?

- **Larger.** They now hold something of their own.
- **Level.** Correct, complete, inert.
- **Smaller.** Instructed, corrected, sold to, or diagnosed and left there.

**Level is the failure state, not the safe state**, and it is where careful
writers land most often. Clearer information is still level.

The test: *after this sentence, what does the reader have that they did not have
before?* If the honest answer is "the information in the sentence," it is level.
Larger means they walk away with evidence about themselves, a pattern they can
use, or a frame that widened.

```
Level      Everything syncs, and there's no storage limit.
Larger     You can stop deleting things to make room.
```

Same fact. The second one reaches into their actual week.

### 3. Would a person say this out loud?

Read it aloud. If you would not say it to someone across a table, cut it.

Straining for lyric produces sentences nobody says. This is its own failure and
it is harder to spot than slop, because it feels like craft while you are doing
it.

```
Affected   Two minutes, before anything else asks for you.
Plain      Takes two minutes.
```

## One position per sentence

A sentence carries one of the three. Trying to make a price line expansive
produces exactly the strained writing test three catches.

- A price, a limit, a deadline: **Regard**. State it, no pressure, stop.
- Anything about what they did: **Sovereignty**.
- Anything about scale, pattern, or what they have not seen yet: **Expanse**.

Match the position to what the sentence is actually for.

## Content patterns

### 1. Trait praise where act praise belongs

**Watch:** *you have a gift for, you're naturally, you're the kind of person
who, your innate, it's just who you are, gifted, talented, a natural.*

**Problem:** Fixed attributions build the fragile kind of pride. Nobody can
repeat a trait. They can repeat an act.

```
Before   You're a natural athlete.
After    You ran in the rain on Tuesday.
```

### 2. Contingent regard

**Watch:** *when you finally, once you've, if you're serious about, ready to
commit, those who truly, the ones who show up.*

**Problem:** Acceptance offered on condition is evaluation wearing warmth. The
reader hears the condition first, and hears that they fall short of it now.

```
Before   For people who are serious about getting fit.
After    Some weeks you'll run four times. Some weeks once.
```

### 3. Diagnosis without return

**Watch:** any statistic about the reader's failure. Streaks broken, sessions
missed, goals unmet, spending over.

**Problem:** Naming a shortfall and stopping leaves someone opened and
abandoned. Complete the arc in the same breath: what it also tells them.

```
Before   You've missed 6 of your last 10 sessions.
After    You've missed six of the last ten. All six were mornings. The four you
         made were after work.
```

The second gives them something to act on. The first gives them a verdict.

### 4. Concepts wearing costumes

**Watch:** any sentence opening *you know that feeling when* or *you know that
pull to,* then naming an abstraction.

**Problem:** A scene shaped sentence containing a definition. It has the grammar
of recognition with none of the content. The reader nods without being seen.

```
Before   You know that feeling when you want to be more productive?
After    You've rewritten the same email three times and still not sent it.
```

**Test:** could a stranger have written this about anyone? Then it is a concept
in costume. Aim for concrete enough to be wrong.

### 5. Counting where naming belongs

**Watch:** a bare quantity offered as a feature. *Over 200 templates, 50+
integrations, 10,000 recipes.*

**Problem:** A count hands the reader a magnitude to measure themselves against,
and flattens distinct things into units. Enumeration also lets a writer avoid
having a view. List five and one might land.

```
Before   Over 200 workout templates.
After    Workouts for a hotel room with no kit, a full gym, or twenty minutes.
```

### 6. Promised feeling

**Watch:** *feel confident, feel calm, feel energised, experience clarity,
unlock your potential, transform your.*

**Problem:** Asserting an emotional outcome puts the reader in debt. If they do
not feel it, they have failed your copy. You cannot install a state by naming
it.

```
Before   Feel energised and confident every morning.
After    Takes about twenty minutes.
```

## Position patterns

### 7. System as subject

**Watch:** any sentence whose grammatical subject is a feature, screen, button
or product name.

**Problem:** The reader becomes a spectator to their own tool. Google, Apple and
Microsoft all put the reader in subject position in their documentation styles.

```
Before   This screen displays your monthly spending.
After    Here's where your money went last month.
```

### 8. Controlling verbs

**Watch:** *must, should, need to, have to, make sure you, don't forget to, be
sure to.*

**Problem:** Pressure costs the reader agency. Autonomy supportive phrasing
gives a reason and leaves the choice with them.

```
Before   You must verify your email before continuing.
After    Verify your email and we can save your work. The link's in your inbox.
```

### 9. Presupposed deficiency

**Watch:** *simply, just, easily, obviously, of course, all you have to do,
don't worry.*

**Problem:** These smuggle in an assumption about the reader. *Simply* implies
anyone competent finds this easy, so struggling means you are not. *Don't worry*
installs the worry.

```
Before   Simply drag your files here to upload.
After    Drag your files here.
```

### 10. Copulas and transactional verbs

**Watch:** *is, means, makes, costs, provides, enables, allows, offers,
features.*

**Problem:** They assert a static relation between two nouns and depict no
event. Nothing happens, so nothing lands.

```
Before   Dark mode is available in Settings.
After    Turn on dark mode in Settings.
```

### 11. Missing stance

**Problem:** Machine prose under-uses hedges, boosters and attitude markers, and
those markers are where affect lives. Strip them and the text is correct,
complete and dead. This is the mechanism behind "robotic", far more than
vocabulary is.

```
Before   Your data is backed up daily.
After    Backed up every night, so a lost phone costs you nothing.
```

### 12. Premature closure

**Problem:** Machine prose resolves to tidy completion in roughly three quarters
of cases whether the material warrants it or not. A sentence that closes
completely leaves no room for the reader's actual situation.

```
Before   Your subscription renews on 4 March.
After    Your subscription renews on 4 March. Change or cancel any time before
         then.
```

## Commercial register

The line is not vocabulary. Advertising is often skilful, playful, genuinely
literary writing. What marks it is an appeal to the addressee with a clock
attached. Sincere registers use the same rhythm and repetition, and decline the
imperative and the deadline.

### 13. Urgency and scarcity

**Watch:** *limited time, only N left, offer ends, act now, don't miss, last
chance, while stocks last.*

```
Before   Only 3 left! Order in the next 2 hours to get it by Friday.
After    Three left in your size.
```

State the fact. Drop the clock. The fact was doing the work anyway.

### 14. Benefit stacking

**Watch:** rapid outcome lists, tick ladders, *plus, unlimited, exclusive
access, everything you need.*

**Problem:** Volume standing in for a claim worth making. Readers skim a stack
and retain none of it.

```
Before   Unlimited storage. Advanced editing. Priority support. Cloud sync.
After    You can stop deleting things to make room.
```

### 15. Superlative and comparison

**Watch:** *best, most powerful, revolutionary, world-class, unlike anything,
the only app that.*

**Problem:** Comparative claims put the reader in a shopping posture. Sincere
registers describe instead of ranking.

```
Before   The world's most advanced note-taking app.
After    Search finds things you forgot you wrote.
```

### 16. Borrowed authority

**Watch:** *science shows, studies prove, backed by science, clinically proven,*
with no study named.

**Problem:** Invoking science without a citation is a costume, and it breaks
badly. A reader who checks and finds nothing discounts everything else you said.

```
Before   Backed by science.
After    The trials ran eight weeks. Most people noticed something around week
         three.
```

## Two failures of craft

### 17. Sentences that should not exist

Covered in test one, listed here so the pass catches them.

**Watch:** instructions for universally known interactions, restatements of a
visible label, claims true of every product, courtesy padding after a complete
thought.

```
Before   Press Enter to send. Hold Shift and Enter for a new line.
After    (nothing)
```

Everyone knows. It costs attention and returns nothing.

### 18. Affectation

**Watch:** inverted word order, a poetic cadence in a functional place, abstract
nouns doing a concrete job, anything you would feel self-conscious saying aloud.

**Problem:** Straining for lyric produces sentences no one says. It feels like
craft while you are doing it, which is what makes it hard to catch.

```
Before   Two minutes, before anything else asks for you.
After    Takes two minutes.
```

**Test:** read it aloud to an imagined person across a table. Flinching means
cut.

## Process

1. Read the whole piece. Mark sentences that fail test one and delete them.
2. If a voice sample exists, read it first and match its sentence lengths,
   punctuation habits, and vocabulary level. Shifting register never means
   overwriting someone's voice with yours.
3. Pass what survives against patterns 1 to 18.
4. Draft.
5. **Audit one.** *"Which of these are level?"* Level is the common failure, not
   smaller. Be honest, especially about lines you like.
6. **Audit two.** *"Which could a stranger have written about anyone?"*
7. **Audit three.** Read it aloud. Mark anything you would not say.
8. Revise against all three.
9. Run the counts.
10. Report.

## The counts

- trait attributions: **zero**
- conditions on acceptance: **zero**
- urgency or scarcity markers: **zero**
- unnamed appeals to science: **zero**
- sentences whose subject is a UI element: **zero**
- shortfalls named without their return: **zero**
- sentences that survived only because they were already written: **zero**
- lines you would not say aloud: **zero**

## Output

1. **Deletions**, with why
2. **Draft**
3. **Audit**, all three passes
4. **Final**
5. **Changes**, a table of each change and which position it serves

## Worked example

A sleep tracking app's onboarding screen.

**Input**

> Unlock your best sleep with the world's most advanced sleep tracker! You're a
> natural night owl, you just haven't optimised your routine yet. Science shows
> that consistent sleep improves everything. Simply wear your device to bed and
> our AI will analyse your patterns. Unlimited history. Advanced insights.
> Priority support. Smart alarms. 70% OFF, next 30 minutes only!

**Deletions**

- "Simply wear your device to bed" — people know how to wear a watch.
- "Science shows that consistent sleep improves everything" — unnamed, and true
  of nothing in particular.
- The four item feature stack — volume standing in for a claim.

**Draft**

> Wear it to bed and you'll see your patterns after a few nights. The annual
> plan is 70% less.

**Audit one, which are level?**

- Both sentences. "You'll see your patterns" is a feature restated. It tells
  them nothing they could not guess from the product's name.

**Audit two, what could a stranger have written about anyone?**

- All of it.

**Audit three, would I say it aloud?**

- Yes, but it is dull. Plain is the floor, not the target.

**Final**

> Most people's worst nights cluster on one weekday. You'll probably know which
> one by Friday.
>
> Wear it to bed. Nothing else to set up.
>
> The annual plan is 70% less.

**Changes**

| Change | Position |
|---|---|
| Opened with a pattern they do not know they have, and a date they can check it | Expanse. Vastness plus something that will not fit the current frame |
| Cut "you're a natural night owl" | Sovereignty. Trait praise builds the fragile kind |
| Cut "you just haven't optimised yet" | Regard. A condition, and a verdict |
| Cut the unnamed science claim | Regard. Borrowed authority is a costume |
| Cut "simply" | Regard. Presupposed their ease |
| Feature stack became one line about setup | Regard. Volume replaced with the only fact that matters at this moment |
| Deadline dropped, price kept | Regard. The fact survives, the clock does not |

Note what the opening does. It is not a claim about the reader, so they cannot
reject it. It sends them to look, and gives them a date by which they will have
their own evidence. What they find belongs to them.
